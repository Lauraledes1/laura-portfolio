A Payments Portal Suppliers Can Check Themselves

Suppliers paid through American Express Supplier Initiated Payments had no way to see their own payments, so they emailed Boost and waited. As the only designer, I designed a portal that answers those questions for them, built it on a design system I started from scratch, and made three trade-offs that decided what ships first, who it serves and how it keeps payment data safe.

Timeline

January–April 2026 to MVP handoff, in progress

Role

Senior Product Designer, sole designer

Method

Proxy interviews, AI-assisted support email analysis, partner reviews

Team

1 Designer + Product + Engineering, reviewed with the American Express team

Context & Opportunity

Buyers pay their vendors with cards they have saved with Boost, suppliers start each payment against an invoice, and Boost runs the payment in the middle. The supplier, the one waiting for the money, is the only party who can't see what is happening, so every question about a payment, a report or a missing invoice number turns into an email to support.

I sorted 808 of those support emails by theme, and 386 of them, 48%, asked something a supplier could answer alone if they could see their own payments. Suppliers said the same thing on sales calls: “Do you have a platform? … or we have to raise a ticket or request on email so you can send us the information.”

How I found what suppliers needed

Proxy interviews

We had no access to American Express's suppliers, so instead of designing blind I interviewed six people doing the same jobs, AR clerks and controllers, at five other supplier companies in Q1 2026. Three personas came out of it, and two of them shaped the second trade-off below.

Support email analysis

I used AI to read and sort 808 supplier emails by the question being asked. Payment status was 19%, remittance reports 17% and invoice matching 12%, which together became the core screens and the launch targets.

Partner reviews

I walked the American Express team through the screens as they took shape. The first review, in January, changed labels we thought were clear: “Paid” hid the difference between authorized and settled, and “Rejected” became “Refunded”, which is what a supplier actually sees.

The foundation

A design system built from scratch

As the only designer across several products, designing every screen from zero wasn't an option, so nobody asked me for a design system and I built one anyway. It started in Figma, moved into code, and now lives in one repository that design and engineering both build from.

Three trade-offs

Trade-off 01 · Scope

Let suppliers see their payments first, and act on them later

Suppliers asked for everything, from creating payments to managing their team and exporting data. The support emails told a narrower story, because the real pain was not knowing where the money was. So the first release only lets suppliers see: the dashboard, payments, payment detail, payers and help, while creating payments, members, reporting, settings and exports moved to phase 2.

We gave up

The most visible features at launch.

We got

A first release aimed at the questions behind 48% of support emails.

Trade-off 02 · Two users

One portal for the power user and the occasional one

The interviews gave me two users pulling in opposite directions. The High-Volume Reconciler matches dozens of payments a day and needs every field, while the Multi-Hat Controller logs in now and then and told me, “I don't want something really busy.” I couldn't build two portals, so I decided what shows first: invoice details sit above the payment method, technical fields like the authorization code fold under Advanced Details, and the dashboard shows five payments with See More instead of an endless list, which also avoided an accessibility problem.

We gave up

One extra click for the power user.

We got

A portal the occasional user doesn't abandon in the first minute.

Trade-off 03 · Security vs. ease of access

One Members screen for three roles, open enough to use and strict enough to be safe

Three people at the same supplier can hold very different powers over payment data. Rather than three versions of the screen, I designed one, where the layout never changes and only the available actions do, so a User who opens a colleague sees the same panel, just locked. Removing someone takes them off the account, while deleting is permanent, shown in red and confirmed first.

Compliance set the barriers, design made them bearable

The roles sit inside a longer flow: how someone gets into the portal, works in it, and leaves it. Each highlighted step is a rule that compliance decided and design had to make livable. The sharpest contrast is at the end, where removing one person is instant but closing a whole company account goes through support, because payment history has to be kept.

What I'd keep, and what I'd do sooner

Ask about compliance in week one

A form for typing in a new card was designed, reviewed and then removed once the card data rules came up. Knowing the rules earlier would have saved that round.

Put real users' words on the screen early

One partner review changed more labels than weeks of internal work, because the words we found clear weren't the words suppliers use.

Build on a system from day one

Designing on shared components meant the portal could carry the partner's brand without hard-coding it into every screen, and the next product starts from the same parts.

Where we're going

Phase 2: let suppliers act

Create Payment, member management, reporting and exports, on the same screens suppliers already know.

Measure what launch changes

Adoption, repeat visits and supplier email volume against the baseline from the support analysis.

Let the next card network partner bring its own brand to the same portal.
